Top 10 AI Tools in the World: What They Do and Who Should Use Them

By MD Shahid Hussain · Information checked: 29 September 2026

An IT professional can ask an assistant to explain a difficult database concept. A trainer can turn rough notes into a lesson outline. A small business can route enquiries to the right person, while a designer can turn an idea into a draft visual. These are ordinary tasks, and AI is increasingly becoming part of how people begin them.

The difficult part is choosing a tool. A chatbot, a coding assistant, a research service, and a video generator solve different problems. Calling one of them the “best AI tool” without saying best for what is rarely useful. The ten options below are a practical cross-category selection, not a measured worldwide ranking by users, revenue, or model performance.

I selected them for their usefulness in real workflows, breadth or depth of capabilities, ease of trying them, and relevance to students, IT professionals, trainers, and business teams. Their availability and free plans were checked against official product and support pages on 29 September 2026. Features, usage caps, and eligibility can change by plan and country.

1. ChatGPT — a versatile place to start

ChatGPT is a conversational assistant for drafting, learning, brainstorming, and working through problems. You describe the task in natural language, then refine the answer with follow-up questions. That makes it useful even when you are unsure how to begin.

Two practical uses: An Oracle Apps DBA can ask for a plain-language explanation of a backup concept before checking vendor documentation. A trainer can turn course objectives into a session outline and discussion questions. It suits students, trainers, analysts, and business professionals who need one adaptable assistant.

Strength: It handles a broad mix of everyday tasks in one interface. Limitation: A convincing answer can still contain errors, so technical instructions and factual claims need independent checking. Free option: Yes; the Free plan offers core access with limits that vary by feature and model. Official plans.

2. Claude — a partner for substantial writing

Claude is a conversational assistant that can write, edit, analyze text and images, generate code, and help structure ideas. It is worth trying when your task involves a long document or several rounds of careful revision.

Two practical uses: A corporate trainer can refine a technical course outline for a nontechnical audience. A product manager can ask it to identify unclear assumptions in a draft product vision. It is suited to writers, educators, consultants, and teams developing detailed documents.

Strength: Its writing and analysis tools support iterative work on complicated material. Limitation: Output still reflects the quality of the prompt and supplied evidence; it cannot validate an untested business assumption on its own. Free option: Yes, with limited usage; more capacity and some capabilities require paid plans. Official plans.

3. Gemini — an everyday assistant for learning and work

Gemini is Google's AI assistant for asking questions, developing ideas, and creating content. It offers a useful alternative when you want to compare how another assistant approaches the same task. Access to advanced models and features depends on your plan and region.

Two practical uses: A student can request a simpler explanation of a machine-learning topic and then ask for a practice example. A business leader can brainstorm questions for a customer interview before turning them into a meeting agenda. It is useful for learners, educators, and general office work.

Strength: It provides a broad entry point to AI assistance. Limitation: Availability and limits can differ across subscription tiers and locations, so check the feature you need before planning a workflow around it. Free option: Yes; the Gemini app has free access, with upgrades for expanded capabilities. Official overview and plan information.

4. Perplexity — a starting point for source-led research

Perplexity combines AI answers with search. Its value is the ability to move from an initial answer to the underlying sources. That is helpful when an answer must be checked or developed into a properly researched article.

Two practical uses: An AI product manager can map the terminology around retrieval-augmented generation and follow the cited material. A trainer can collect starting sources for a new course module before building slides. It suits researchers, writers, students, and people beginning market discovery.

Strength: It makes source exploration convenient. Limitation: A citation does not automatically prove that the linked page supports every statement in the answer; open and read important sources yourself. Free option: Yes; the Standard plan includes basic searches, with tight limits on advanced searches and research. Official plan guide.

5. GitHub Copilot — assistance inside the coding workflow

GitHub Copilot helps developers with code suggestions and chat while they work. Unlike a general chatbot used separately from an editor, it is designed around the development process.

Two practical uses: A Python learner can get a first pass at a data-cleaning function, then inspect and test it. A web developer can ask for a starting point for a responsive component and adjust it to the site's style. It suits developers and technically inclined learners who can review generated code.

Strength: Suggestions appear close to where code is written. Limitation: Generated code may be insecure, inefficient, or wrong for the project; review and tests remain essential. Free option: Yes; GitHub lists monthly limits for completions and chat on Copilot Free. Official plans.

6. Canva AI — faster first drafts for visual communication

Canva AI brings AI-assisted creation into Canva's visual design environment. It can help people move from a brief to an initial design, then edit the result for their own audience and brand.

Two practical uses: A training team can draft a workshop poster for LinkedIn. A consultant can build a presentation visual that explains a service in simpler terms. It is especially useful for trainers, marketers, entrepreneurs, and people who regularly produce social or presentation content.

Strength: AI assistance sits alongside familiar design tools. Limitation: Generated layouts and images still need human review for accuracy, readability, originality, and brand fit. Free option: Yes; Canva says a range of AI tools can be tried on the Free plan, while higher usage and advanced tools require paid access. Official Canva AI page.

7. Grammarly — clearer everyday writing

Grammarly helps with written communication through feedback and AI-assisted drafting. Its value is practical: improving a sentence that already exists can matter more than generating another long document.

Two practical uses: A job applicant can improve the clarity and tone of a cover email. A trainer can polish a course announcement before sending it to corporate participants. It suits anyone who writes frequent emails, posts, proposals, or learning materials in English.

Strength: It focuses attention on the quality of everyday writing. Limitation: Suggestions can flatten a distinctive voice or change intended meaning, so accept edits selectively. Free option: Yes; its official AI writing page lists a limited monthly prompt allowance on Free. Official feature page.

8. Notion AI — assistance where team knowledge lives

Notion AI works within Notion's workspace for notes, documents, and projects. The appeal is context: when a team already keeps its work in Notion, AI assistance can be used close to those materials.

Two practical uses: A product team can turn rough discovery notes into a structured draft for review. A training coordinator can organize programme information and prepare an initial summary for colleagues. It suits teams already using Notion for documentation and collaboration.

Strength: It can fit into an existing knowledge workflow. Limitation: Its usefulness depends on the quality and organization of the workspace; an incomplete knowledge base leads to incomplete answers. Free option: Notion has a Free workspace plan, but AI access on it is a limited trial, not unrestricted ongoing AI use. Official pricing.

9. Zapier — turning repeatable steps into workflows

Zapier connects apps and automates actions between them. It is especially relevant when work involves repeated handoffs: someone fills in a form, information goes into a table, and a colleague needs an alert. AI-related features can assist with some workflows, but access varies by plan and feature.

Two practical uses: A training business can send new enquiry details from a form to a tracking table. A small support team can trigger a notification when a new request arrives. It suits operations, marketing, training coordination, and small business teams with repeatable processes.

Strength: It links work across applications without building every integration from scratch. Limitation: A broken trigger, bad input, or poorly designed process can automate mistakes; monitor the results. Free option: Yes, for basic automation with task limits. However, Zapier says a Free user can preview an AI by Zapier step in an unpublished workflow but needs an eligible paid plan to publish that AI step. Official pricing and AI-step guidance.

10. Runway — exploring AI-generated video

Runway offers tools for generating and editing visual content. It can help a creator test a concept before investing in a full production process, though the exact models available depend on the plan.

Two practical uses: A trainer can prototype a short visual opener for a course. A marketing team can explore a storyboard direction for a campaign before commissioning final assets. It suits video creators, educators, and visual marketing teams prepared to review outputs closely.

Strength: It lowers the effort needed to experiment with visual ideas. Limitation: The free plan is for exploration, with restricted models and watermarked video; repeated production will usually require a paid plan. Free option: Yes, with a one-time credit allocation that does not refresh monthly. Official free-plan details.

How should you choose?

Begin with the task you repeat most often. If you need to understand a subject or produce a first draft, try a general assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. If you need sources, begin with Perplexity and read the source pages. If you write code, test GitHub Copilot in a real project. For visual assets, compare Canva AI with Runway according to whether you need a design or a video concept. Choose Grammarly for sentence-level writing help, Notion AI when your existing work lives in Notion, and Zapier when the problem is a repeatable handoff between apps.

Use the free options to test one real task, not ten impressive demos. Ask whether the output saves time after you verify and edit it. Check how the tool handles sensitive information before adding client data, and confirm current plan limits before making it part of a business process. These are practical selection principles rather than product guarantees.

Final thought

AI tools are most valuable when paired with clear judgment. A good assistant can accelerate a draft, but it cannot replace your responsibility for a correct database procedure, a trustworthy research claim, an accessible design, or a customer promise. Pick a problem, try the relevant tool, check the result, and keep what actually improves your work.
